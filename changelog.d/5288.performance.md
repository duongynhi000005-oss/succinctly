- **jq: a fold step whose operand reads the state into scalars is linear** (#5288). `reduce range(N) as $k ({i:0,a:[]}; .a += [.i])` cost the size of the whole state on every step, so it was quadratic where jq is linear: the operand was evaluated on a copy of the state that was serialised and indexed again. The step now reads `.i`, `.a += [.i, .j]`'s fields, a comparison such as `.n > 3`, `not`/`type` of a field, and `+`, `-` or `*` of integers (up to 2^53, the range jq computes exactly) straight off the tree, for integers, booleans, `null` and strings. A float, a container read off the state, a division and a string concatenation take the earlier route, so their spelling and errors are unchanged. Measured on an Apple M5 Max and an AMD Ryzen 9 7950X (release builds, interleaved, min of 5, output identical to jq 1.7.1): `.a += [.i]` at 10,000 / 20,000 / 40,000 steps 290 / 1,080 / 4,426 ms → 9 / 10 / 16 ms on the M5 Max and 395 / 1,564 / 6,193 ms → 5 / 9 / 16 ms on the 7950X (jq at 10,000: 120 ms); `.a += [.i + 1]` at 40,000 steps 8,055 ms → 22 ms and 11,250 ms → 21 ms; 2,000 steps beside an untouched 100,000-element field 3,394 ms → 7 ms and 3,775 ms → 7 ms; the closed-operand control `.a += [$k]` and the float row `.a += [.f + 1]` are unchanged.
